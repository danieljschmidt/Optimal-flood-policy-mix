# Optimal Flood Policy — Project State, Strategic Direction, and Next Steps

*Covers: main draft (April 24), empirical-counterparts note (April 23), elasticity literature note (April 25), adaptation-model note (May), adaptation-capital evidence note (July 12), two-region note (March), and Gruber & Solomon, NBER WP 35408 (July 2026).*

---

## 1. Executive assessment

The project has a genuinely novel mechanism, most of the theory it needs, a sourced (if error-ridden) calibration, and — as of July — a large, well-executed NBER working paper on adjacent territory. Gruber–Solomon (G&S) does not kill the project; it kills one *version* of it, namely a paper whose headline is "what is the optimal flood insurance subsidy?". G&S optimize the subsidy $s$ while explicitly holding disaster-relief generosity fixed, flag relief generosity as the single most influential parameter in their robustness analysis, and treat behavioral frictions as a uniform scalar wedge in an appendix. Those are exactly the gaps our machinery — the belief distribution $F(q)$, the endogenous take-up margin $q^*$, and the instrument-asymmetric behavioral responses — is built to fill.

The binding constraints are no longer conceptual. They are: several concrete errors that must be fixed before any number is trusted (§5); a fragmented calibration in which six parameters take different values across documents; the mandate and heterogeneous-$p$ realism gaps; the belief-identification problem, which deserves its own defense plan (§7); and speed, since the G&S paper will attract follow-on work on the relief margin. The new adaptation-capital note (July 12) removes one of these blockers outright — it supplies the cost data and annuitization convention the adaptation module was missing — and adds two empirical moments (aid over-perception, aid→adaptation crowd-out) that validate model mechanisms we previously could only assert.

## 2. What Gruber–Solomon changes

### 2.1 Territory now occupied

G&S own the following, with better data than we can match: the optimal *subsidy level* as a sufficient-statistics exercise (their answer: 52%, near the pre-RR2.0 status quo, with infinite MVPF for the first 8 points); the first credible estimate of the insurance→relief fiscal offset ($173–$216 per house per 1pp coverage, IV'd off neighbor-flood salience, implying FEMA covers $f \approx 13.3\%$ of uninsured losses); a within-property demand elasticity of −0.32 from FOIA'd billed-vs-full-risk premium data; reclassification risk from climate-model disagreement (a channel we do not have and should not chase); and directly estimated mitigation ($\eta_m \approx 0.34$ in Florida, ≈0.075 reweighted nationally) and location ($\eta_x \approx 0.0077$) moral-hazard elasticities. Any framing of our paper as "how large should flood insurance subsidies be" now reads as a worse-identified replication.

### 2.2 Territory explicitly left open — and pointed at

Three doors are left open, and G&S themselves gesture at each. First, **relief is never a choice variable**: their Panel C shows $s^*$ swinging from 52% to 15% as FEMA generosity varies, and their conclusion concedes that FEMA reform "would substantially affect our conclusions" — but their framework cannot say what relief generosity *should be*, nor compare the marginal public dollar across instruments. Second, **beliefs enter only as a uniform wedge** ($k_R = 0.57$ applied to everyone, in an appendix), which pushes their optimal subsidy to a corner (89–100%). A uniform wedge has no interior logic: if everyone underperceives, subsidize everything. Our heterogeneous $F(q)$ generates the interior trade-off endogenously and yields a result their setup cannot express — there is always a low-$q$ tail that no finite subsidy reaches, and relief is the only instrument that insures households whose beliefs make them unreachable by price incentives. Third, **no housing-market incidence**: everything is partial equilibrium, despite their own migration evidence, which keeps the two-region companion-paper option alive.

### 2.3 What we should import rather than compete with

Their estimates resolve most of our calibration TODOs and should be adopted as inputs, with attribution: $\eta_q = -0.32$ (voluntary; −0.25 in SFHA, confirming mandate attenuation), $L_f = \$29{,}267$, $\bar P = \$1{,}739$, intensive margin ≈ 0 (which independently justifies our binary insurance choice), $\eta_x = 0.0077$ (which lets us bound the sorting threat in one paragraph instead of building the spatial model), $\eta_m$ for the adaptation module, and the administrative-cost asymmetry — NFIP overhead ≈ 30% of premiums vs. FEMA ≈ 13% of assistance. That last number deserves emphasis: G&S treat admin costs as a robustness check that trims $s^*$ to 40%; in our two-instrument framework it is a first-order argument in the MVPF comparison, and it tilts toward relief. Their $f \approx 13.3\%$ versus our IA-grant-based $a \approx 4$–$7\%$ must be reconciled, not averaged (§6).

Their consistency with our numbers is also worth stating plainly: $s^* = 52\%$ at a current $s \approx 47\%$ is the statement that MVPF$_s \approx 1$ at the status quo — which is what our corrected local evaluation finds (MVPF$_s \approx 1.00$–$1.05$). No contradiction anywhere; they simply never compute MVPF$_a$, and our preliminary MVPF$_a \approx 1.36$ is the number their paper is structurally unable to produce.

## 3. The repositioned paper

**Working thesis.** With heterogeneous flood-risk beliefs, ex ante subsidies and ex post relief are not substitutes along a single Samaritan's-dilemma margin; they are *complements that serve different segments of the belief distribution*. Subsidies move the take-up margin $q^*$ and correct the internality of moderately misperceiving households; relief insures the deep-underperceiving tail that no affordable subsidy reaches. Crucially, relief's moral-hazard cost is self-attenuating exactly when beliefs are the friction: crowd-out scales with $q^*$ while subsidy crowd-in scales with $p$, so the households most tempted to free-ride on relief are the ones who barely believe floods will happen. The optimal policy therefore uses both instruments, and at current US policy the marginal dollar does better as relief than as additional subsidy.

**Contribution relative to G&S**, to be stated in the introduction roughly as: G&S establish that, holding FEMA fixed, high premium subsidies are justified by fiscal spillovers and reclassification risk; we endogenize the relief margin they hold fixed, replace their uniform misperception wedge with a belief distribution identified from take-up data, and characterize the optimal instrument mix. Where their behavioral extension drives the subsidy to a corner, belief heterogeneity restores an interior optimum with a well-defined division of labor across instruments. This is complement positioning, and it should be made unmistakable: cite them in the first two paragraphs, and consider sending them the draft — they thank Mulder and Collier, so the network is adjacent.

**Three headline deliverables.** (i) The theory result: instrument-asymmetric behavioral responses under belief heterogeneity — $\partial I/\partial s \propto p\,u'(c_I)/\Delta u$ vs. $\partial I/\partial a \propto q^* u'(c_{U,F})/\Delta u$ — with the exact marginal internality $(p-q^*)\Delta u$, nesting G&S's uniform wedge as the degenerate-$F$ case. (ii) The local evaluation: MVPF$_s$ vs. MVPF$_a$ (vs. MVPF$_b$) at current US policy, with corrected behavioral terms and the admin-cost wedge. (iii) The global exercise: the optimal $(s, a, b)$ frontier over the fitted belief distribution, including a decomposition figure showing which part of $F(q)$ each instrument serves — this figure is the paper's visual signature and has no counterpart in G&S.

A secondary theme now within reach, thanks to the adaptation-capital note: instruments also divide labor across the *damage distribution*. Dry-floodproofing eliminates losses up to its design height and nothing beyond; elevation shifts the inundation-depth distribution; insurance alone covers the tail. Once continuous damage is the baseline, adaptation and insurance are partial complements rather than pure substitutes — protecting the body and the tail of $G(D)$ respectively — which parallels how subsidies and relief serve different parts of $F(q)$. This should be developed only as far as one proposition and one figure; it enriches the mix story without displacing it.

## 4. Asset-by-asset evaluation against this direction

**Main draft.** The skeleton survives intact — the model, the MVPF derivations, the $\Delta u$-cancellation, and the $f(q^*)$ recovery are all load-bearing for the new framing. The motivation must be rewritten around the mix question and the G&S contrast, and the continuous-damage extension gets promoted to baseline — now doubly necessary, since the adaptation technologies in the July note (threshold protection, depth shift) are only expressible against a damage distribution. The risk-aversion-heterogeneity section becomes a short robustness subsection — its finding that dispersion in $\gamma$ barely matters is useful precisely because it clears the stage for beliefs. Substantive corrections required in this document are detailed in §5.

**Empirical-counterparts note.** Keep the architecture, replace the numbers. Beyond the units error explained in §5, adopt G&S's $\eta_q = -0.32$ as the baseline elasticity (retiring the −0.17 unadjusted semi-elasticity), rebuild the relief parameter per §6, and unify the parameter values that currently disagree across documents.

**Adaptation-model note (May).** Now more valuable, not less: G&S estimate mitigation *responses* but have no adaptation *instrument*, while real policy (HMGP, FMA, BRIC, and RR2.0's mitigation discounts) does. Integrate the discrete model and the premium-adjusted variant, whose result $\partial k^{IA}/\partial s < 0$ — insurance subsidies crowd out adaptation by the insured — is the theoretical counterpart of what G&S measure. The continuous-adaptation case goes to an appendix. Repairs required first are in §5; the calibration now comes from the July note below.

**Adaptation-capital evidence note (July 12).** This note closes the adaptation module's empirical gap and should be adopted nearly wholesale, with four uses and three caveats. Uses: (i) **Costs.** Aerts et al. (2019) FEMA-based columns give per-building costs by measure, height, and building class; following the note, drop wet-proofing, scale NYC-derived figures by 0.75 and everything to current dollars. (ii) **Annuitization convention.** The note's 80-year effective life and ~4% discount rate imply an annualization factor of about 4.2% of upfront cost — exactly the user-cost fix flagged in §5, now with a defensible source rather than an arbitrary choice. (iii) **Damage technology.** Replace the proportional-damage-reduction parameter $\delta$ with the note's specification: dry-proofing as full protection up to an overtopping threshold, elevation as a leftward shift of the inundation-depth distribution, households choosing over {none, dry-proof, elevate} at discrete heights. This is more credible than $\delta$, integrates with the continuous-damage baseline, and generates the body-vs-tail complementarity in §3. (iv) **Validation moments.** The Botzen et al. (2019) survey delivers two findings that map directly onto model objects: respondents over-estimate federal aid (32% expect aid covering about half their damage — against actual generosity of 5–13%, direct evidence for aid over-perception and broadly consistent with G&S's $k_F = 1.5$ calibration, arguably conservative); and aid expectations are *negatively* related to elevation uptake — a direct empirical counterpart to our comparative static $\partial q^{**}/\partial a > 0$ (relief crowds out adaptation). This is the first observed moment that speaks to a relief-side behavioral response in our model, and it belongs in the validation section, not a footnote.

Caveats: the cost data are NYC/LA circa 2010–2012, so external validity rests on the 0.75 scaling and inflation adjustment — present cost sensitivity, not point estimates. Fried (REStud 2022) does not measure adaptation capital directly (it calibrates an effectiveness parameter within an assumed functional form); use its 1.17%-of-capital figure only as a macro plausibility check and its $F(a) = \exp(-\theta a^{1/\theta})$ form as the appendix continuous-technology benchmark. Most importantly, the note surfaces a distinction it does not itself draw out: elevating an *existing* home costs \$44k–\$92k while elevating at *construction* costs \$1.4k–\$7.4k — a factor of ten to thirty. Annuitized, new-build elevation (\$60–\$310/yr) is trivially worthwhile against premiums of \$1.7k–\$4.5k, while retrofits (\$1.4k–\$3.9k/yr) are marginal even at high-risk premiums. This reframes the evidence base: G&S's elevation certificates are filed *prior to building*, so their $\eta_m \approx 0.34$ is essentially a new-construction elasticity, while Collier et al.'s finding that only 6.6% of lapsed high-risk policies mitigated reflects the expensive retrofit margin. The adaptation module should carry both margins — cheap-elastic new builds, expensive-inelastic retrofits — with MVPF$_b$ computed separately, since real-world adaptation subsidies (HMGP/FMA) fund retrofits while price incentives operate on new construction. That two-margin structure is itself a small contribution: it explains why measured mitigation elasticities range from large (G&S, Florida new builds) to near-zero (Collier retrofits; the null results G&S themselves cite) without invoking different behavioral models.

**Elasticity literature note.** Its main job is now done by G&S's estimate, but it retains three uses: the elasticity range for robustness ($[-0.17, -1]$, with the Collier kink and the cumulative-vs-flow distinction as caveats); Mulder's information effect (floodplain designation raises take-up 17–30pp holding premiums fixed) as the crucial second moment for validating the fitted belief distribution (§7); and the salience critique — Collier's non-renewals tracking media coverage rather than prices — as an alternative-interpretation threat treated in §7. Verify the ‡ entries (Bradt, Wagner, Gourevitch, Ortega–Petkov, Browne–Hoyt, Atreya) against the originals before citing.

**Two-region note.** Defer, with a cleaner exit than before: G&S's $\eta_x = 0.0077$ — an order of magnitude below their mitigation response, contributing $0.04 per house-year to their fiscal calculus — is the empirical bound we planned to construct ourselves. One paragraph plus one back-of-envelope number ("a migration elasticity would need to be roughly X times G&S's estimate to overturn MVPF$_a$ > MVPF$_s$") replaces the entire spatial model in this paper. The full model survives as a promising *companion paper* on incidence: G&S are explicitly partial-equilibrium in housing, their own cited evidence shows near-full capitalization of premium changes into prices (contradicting elastic supply), and "who really receives flood subsidies — households or coastal landowners?" is an open question the two-region framework with inelastic supply is built for.

## 5. Errors and required fixes

These are ordered by how much they threaten the paper's conclusions. Each is described so it can be assigned and fixed without re-reading the source documents.

**The elasticity units error (empirical-counterparts note).** The note defines $\varepsilon \equiv (1/I)(\partial I/\partial \pi)$ — a *level* derivative with units of one-over-premium — and plugs in $-0.17$ from the literature. But the literature's $-0.17$ is an elasticity or semi-elasticity: a percent-for-percent (or percentage-point-per-percent) object. The two differ by a factor of $1/\pi$, and with the premium $\pi = (1-s)pd \approx 0.0016$ in wealth units, that factor is roughly 600. The note's resulting take-up response, $\partial I/\partial s = 0.000153$, implies that a premium increase equal to *the household's entire wealth* would reduce take-up by 5 percentage points — an absurdity that flags the error immediately. The draft's own equation (16) has the correct convention, $\partial I/\partial \pi = \varepsilon I / \pi$; the note simply failed to apply it. The fix is mechanical (recompute $f(q^*) = -\varepsilon (I/\pi)(\Delta u / u'(c_I)) \approx 5.3$ at $\varepsilon = -0.17$, and rerun the MVPFs), but the consequences are not: the corrected behavioral terms are 10–20% effects at low $|\varepsilon|$ and first-order at $|\varepsilon| = 1$, so the note's claim that internality and fiscal-externality terms are "negligibly small" is false and must be retracted. Preliminary corrected numbers — MVPF$_s \approx 1.05$, MVPF$_a \approx 1.36$ — suggest the ranking survives, but this must be established on a clean, single codebase, not asserted from a hand calculation.

**The crowd-out dominance claim (main draft, take-up responses section).** The draft asserts that because $q^* < p$, relief-induced crowd-out is "always weaker" than the crowd-in from an equal-cost subsidy. The comparison actually pits $q^* d\, u'(c_{U,F})$ against $p d\, u'(c_I)$, and since uninsured flood victims are poorer, $u'(c_{U,F}) > u'(c_I)$ works *against* the claim. The correct sufficient condition is $q^*/p < u'(c_I)/u'(c_{U,F})$, which holds comfortably in our calibration (small $pd$, moderate $d$) but not universally. Two further qualifications belong in the same passage: "equal-cost" comparisons must weight by the recipient populations $I$ versus $1-I$, and $q^* < p$ itself is not guaranteed globally — generous relief with a small subsidy can push the threshold above $p$, at which point the internality signs flip. The theorem should be restated with its actual conditions and the parameter region made explicit; as it stands, a referee will find the counterexample in an afternoon.

**Budget derivatives in the adaptation-model note (equations 52 and 73).** Two of the fiscal derivatives in the continuous and premium-adjusted sections do not survive term-by-term checking. In (52), the derivative of the budget with respect to the adaptation subsidy $b$, the relief-savings term from induced adaptation appears without its $ap$ prefactor, and the term $b \int k'_b f\,dq$ — the extra subsidy bill from *existing* adapters raising their investment — is missing entirely. In (73), the derivative with respect to $s$ under premium-adjusted insurance, the middle bracket is dimensionally inconsistent (a factor of $d$ appears to have been dropped) and the fiscal saving on relief when households switch across the insurance margin is absent. The welfare-side derivations (envelope arguments, internality wedges) check out; it is specifically the budget side that needs a full re-derivation before MVPF$_b$ can be computed. Since the three-instrument table is the paper's centerpiece, this is blocking.

**The adaptation cost is not annuitized (adaptation-model note, condition 13).** The model's condition for a non-empty adapt region, $(1-b)k < (1-s)pd$, compares a *one-shot capital cost* $k$ against a *one-year premium*. At the raw magnitudes in the July note — retrofit elevation at \$44k–\$92k against annual premiums near \$1.7k — the condition fails and the adapt region is empty by construction. The July note supplies the resolution: an 80-year effective life and a ~4% discount rate imply a user cost of roughly 4.2% of the capital outlay per year, under which dry-proofing (≈\$350–\$430/yr) and new-build elevation (≈\$60–\$310/yr) comfortably clear the threshold and retrofits (≈\$1.4k–\$3.9k/yr) sit near the margin — which is exactly the empirically observed pattern. Rewrite the model with $k$ as an annualized user cost, cite the note's sources for the convention, and the previously vacuous condition becomes an empirically disciplined selection margin.

**The budget constraint typo (main draft, equation 6).** As typeset, the government budget reads $B = pds\,I + a(1 - I)$: the relief term has lost its $pd$ factor, so relief spending is not multiplied by the probability and size of the loss it compensates. The Lagrangian and FOCs that follow use the correct expression, so this is a typo rather than a propagated error — but it is a typo in the paper's central constraint.

**Mandate contamination of the belief recovery (main draft, identification section).** The recovery of $f(q^*)$ from $(I, \varepsilon)$ assumes all take-up is voluntary, i.e. $1 - I = F(q^*)$. Mulder's adjustment implies roughly 54% of SFHA policyholders hold insurance only because of the lender mandate, and G&S's elasticity split (−0.25 inside the SFHA vs. −0.32 overall) confirms the attenuation. Mandated households buy regardless of $q$, so both moments are contaminated: $I$ overstates voluntary demand and $\varepsilon$ understates voluntary price sensitivity. This is not a bug in the algebra but a misspecification that biases the recovered belief density and hence every internality term. The fix is a mandated segment in the model — a fraction of households (concentrated in high-$p$ zones) whose take-up is exogenous — with the belief recovery run on the voluntary segment and voluntary elasticity, and the subsidy to mandated households booked as a pure inframarginal transfer.

**Common true risk $p$ (main draft, throughout).** With a single $p$ for everyone, all non-take-up is a belief mistake by construction, which mechanically inflates the internality and the case for subsidies. True flood risk varies by orders of magnitude even within the SFHA, and non-take-up by genuinely low-$p$ households is rational. At minimum a two-zone version (high-$p$/low-$p$, calibrated to SFHA/non-SFHA) is needed; it interacts with the mandate fix above, since the mandate binds precisely where $p$ is high.

**Consistency and housekeeping.** Six parameters currently take different values across documents and must be unified in one master calibration file: $p$ (0.01 in the draft, 0.02 in the note), $d/w$ (0.15 in both computations, but 0.5 in the note's own table caption), $s$ (0.30 vs. 0.47), $I$ (0.50 vs. 0.30), $a$ (0.05 vs. 0.055 vs. 0.074), and the Mulder citation (2021 vs. 2024 — vintages of the same working paper; cite the 2024 version). The draft also references a non-existent "Figure 3.2," uses $s = 13.9\%$ and $a = 7.4\%$ before defining them as the baseline optimum, and reports $\sigma_q$ in percent while the figure axis reports var$_q$ in levels. Adaptation cost figures must additionally be tagged by vintage and location (NYC 2012 vs. LA 2010 dollars) before the 0.75 and inflation scalings are applied, so the provenance of every number in the eventual calibration table is auditable.

## 6. Calibration reconciliation with G&S

The relief parameter needs to be rebuilt rather than picked. G&S's $f \approx 0.133$ is inferred from their spillover coefficient and bundles IHP grants ($183 of the $216), SBA loans, HMGP, and GSE channels; our $a \approx 4$–$7\%$ counts IA grants only. The right move is to let the model distinguish the **household-benefit rate** from the **fiscal-cost rate** of relief: SBA disaster loans deliver less than face value to households (they must be repaid) while costing the government roughly 13 cents per dollar, so the two rates genuinely diverge. Introducing a relief wedge ($a_{\text{benefit}} \ne a_{\text{cost}}$) is a small extension with three payoffs: it reconciles the two numbers, it is where the NFIP-30%-vs-FEMA-13% administrative asymmetry naturally lives, and it feeds directly into the MVPF comparison that is our headline. Run the headline table under both $a$-conventions; the expectation is that a larger effective $a$ raises MVPF$_s$ somewhat (bigger relief savings per crowd-in, via the $(s-a)$ term) while also raising the fiscal cost of relief, leaving the qualitative ranking intact — but this must be computed, not assumed.

For beliefs about relief, the Botzen survey adds a third data point to the triangulation: households' *perceived* generosity (roughly half of damage, among the 32% expecting aid) sits far above both the IA-grant rate and G&S's $f$, supporting an over-perception wedge at least as large as G&S's $k_F = 1.5$ and giving our model's perceived-$a$ a direct empirical anchor distinct from actual-$a$ (developed in §7.4). Remaining calibration anchors: heterogeneous $p$ across a high-risk/low-risk split rather than a single 0.01/0.02; $d/w$ as a range 0.10–0.40 with the wealth-of-flood-victims caveat; adaptation costs and technologies from Aerts et al. (2019) as specified in §4, split by new-build and retrofit margins; and beliefs validated against $k_R = 0.57$ (Bakkensen–Barrage, which G&S also use) — our fitted $F(q)$ should be shown to imply a mean underperception of roughly that size, a free credibility check.

## 7. Belief identification: the central vulnerability and the defense plan

### 7.1 The problem, stated precisely

Every welfare statement in the paper flows through one interpretive act: the pair $(I, \varepsilon)$ — voluntary take-up and its premium sensitivity — is read as tracing out the belief distribution, via $1 - I = F(q^*)$ and $f(q^*) = -\varepsilon (I/\pi)(\Delta u/u'(c_I))$. That recovery attributes *all* non-take-up and *all* price sensitivity to heterogeneous subjective probabilities. The internality correction $(p - q^*)\Delta u$ — the term that makes subsidies more than a transfer and relief's crowd-out less than a full welfare cost — is only valid if the marginal non-buyer is genuinely *mistaken about risk*. Several rival frictions generate the same observables with different, sometimes opposite, welfare content. This is not a technicality: it is the objection every serious referee will raise first, and the paper must be built so that the objection has a designated place to land and a quantified answer waiting for it.

### 7.2 The confounders, and what each would imply if it — not beliefs — drove non-take-up

| Confounder | Shared signature with low $q$ | Welfare content if it dominates | Discriminating evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Liquidity constraints | Low take-up; renewal sensitive to price | Subsidy still valuable, but as constraint relief, not internality correction; relief becomes *more* attractive (state-contingent liquidity when marginal utility is highest) | Collier: no gradient of non-renewal in tract income or in premium-hike size; but Gourevitch: larger RR2.0 responses in low-income ZIPs | Unresolved; handled by contamination-share robustness (§7.5) |
| Claim distrust, coverage caps, payout delays | Rational WTP below actuarial value even at $q = p$ | Internality overstated; part of the subsidy is a transfer toward a product households rationally discount | Mulder's information effect: risk information alone raises take-up 17–30pp, hard to square with distrust as the binding friction; Wagner and Bradt find no adverse selection | Partially defused; residual folded into a perceived-load parameter |
| Salience / attention (Collier) | Take-up responses track media coverage; kinked, saturating demand | Recovered $f(q^*)$ conflates belief density with an attention hazard; $F$ is not stable, so *global* counterfactuals are misspecified | Prefer Mulder's $\varepsilon$ (information held constant within the newly-mapped population) for the belief mapping; Gallagher's post-flood decay | Contained locally, owned globally (§7.5) |
| Rational relief anticipation | Non-take-up is optimal given expected aid | Not a confounder at all once $a$ is an instrument — it is the model's own mechanism | Botzen: perceived aid ≈ 50% of damage vs. actual 5–13% | Converted into a mechanism, with a twist (§7.4) |

Two further rivals — heterogeneous true risk $p$ and premium loads — are handled as model corrections in §5 (the two-zone extension and the admin-load wedge) rather than as identification threats, because they are observable enough to build in directly.

The table's welfare column is the reason this section exists: the confounders do not merely add noise, they *reallocate* the welfare argument. If liquidity dominates, the case for relief strengthens and the case for subsidies changes its rationale; if distrust dominates, the subsidy internality shrinks toward zero; if salience dominates, the local evaluation survives but the global frontier loses its footing. A paper that cannot say which regime it is in must instead show its conclusions are robust across regimes — which is the architecture below.

### 7.3 The defense: over-identification rather than assertion

The Beta specification for $F(q)$ has two free parameters. We have, or can construct, four moments, which turns the belief interpretation from an assumption into a testable restriction. First, the **voluntary take-up level** (after the mandated segment is removed per §5) pins the mass below $q^*$. Second, the **voluntary premium elasticity** pins the density at $q^*$ — using Mulder's information-constant design as the preferred estimate precisely because it is the one least contaminated by salience. Third, **Mulder's information effect** is the over-identifying moment: floodplain designation, holding premiums fixed, raises take-up by 17–30pp. In the model this is a belief shock — a shift of $q$ toward $p$ for the newly designated — and the fitted $F$ makes a quantitative prediction for the induced take-up response. If the fitted density cannot rationalize a jump of that magnitude, the beliefs-only model is *rejected*, and honestly reporting that test is worth more than passing it quietly. Fourth, **survey moments** validate the level: Bakkensen–Barrage's elicited mean underperception ($k_R = 0.57$, the same figure G&S adopt) should be approximately reproduced by the fitted $F$'s implied mean $q/p$, and the FEMA National Household Survey and Botzen elicitations provide additional external checks. The discipline throughout: surveys are used as *validation targets, not calibration inputs*, so the revealed-preference identification stands on its own and the surveys certify it.

### 7.4 Perceived versus actual relief: the converted confounder

Rational relief anticipation deserves separate treatment because our framework changes its status. In a subsidy-only model (G&S's), households declining insurance because they expect aid is a confounder to be assumed away or cited around. In ours, the threshold $q^*(s, a)$ already embeds it. But the Botzen moment exposes a wedge the current model misses: behavior responds to *perceived* generosity $a_{\text{perc}}$ (≈ half of damage, among those expecting aid), which sits far above actual generosity (5–13%). Distinguishing the two — $a_{\text{perc}}$ in the household's threshold condition, actual $a$ in the budget and in realized consumption — has three consequences worth building in.

First, **identification**: recovering $F(q)$ under actual $a$ when households act on $a_{\text{perc}} > a$ misattributes the relief-anticipation wedge to pessimistic beliefs. Since a higher perceived $a$ lowers $\Delta u$ and raises $q^*$ for given choices, the corrected recovery shifts the fitted beliefs *upward* — the belief gap $p - q$ shrinks, and part of what looked like a risk-misperception internality is reclassified as a *relief-misperception* internality (G&S's $k_F$ wedge, for which Botzen now gives a direct anchor). Total behavioral justification for intervention is similar; its composition, and hence its comparative statics, are not. Second, **a new result direction**: if perceptions of aid are anchored and update weakly with policy — the Botzen numbers suggest perceptions bear little relation to actual program parameters — then marginal changes in *actual* $a$ induce little additional crowd-out, because the behavioral margin is keyed to $a_{\text{perc}}$, while the consumption-smoothing benefit of actual relief accrues in full. This compounds with the $q^* < p$ dampening: relief's moral-hazard cost is attenuated twice, once because the marginal households underweight floods and once because they don't track relief generosity anyway. Both dampenings push the same way as our headline, and the second is novel relative to everything in G&S. Third, **a required caveat**: a planner who raises actual $a$ toward perceived levels may eventually re-anchor perceptions (large visible disasters plausibly do exactly this, per Gallagher-style salience), so the attenuation result should be presented as a short-run property with the re-anchoring risk stated, not as a free lunch.

### 7.5 Robustness architecture: making the residual vulnerability quantitative

Whatever the tests in §7.3 show, some contamination survives, and the paper should price it rather than argue it away. The central device is a **contamination-share exercise**: let $\chi$ be the fraction of observed voluntary non-take-up attributable to non-belief frictions (rational households at $q = p$ who abstain for liquidity, distrust, or load reasons). Re-run the belief recovery on the remaining $1 - \chi$, recompute all MVPFs as functions of $\chi$, and report the breakeven $\chi^*$ at which the MVPF$_a$–MVPF$_s$ ranking flips. This converts an unwinnable qualitative dispute ("which friction dominates?") into a transparent sensitivity statement ("the ranking survives unless more than $\chi^*$ of non-take-up is non-belief-driven"), and a referee who disputes the belief interpretation must then argue for a specific $\chi > \chi^*$, which is a much harder position than generic skepticism.

Around that device, three supporting practices. **Dual elasticities**: report every headline result under both Mulder's information-constant $\varepsilon$ and G&S's within-property $\eta_q$, so the salience critique is visibly priced. **Exposure grading**: state explicitly that the local evaluation needs only the voluntary take-up level and the density at the margin — it is exposed to *mislabeling* the margin but not to the global shape or stability of $F$ — while the $(s, a, b)$ frontier needs the full distribution and its stability, and is therefore presented with functional-form robustness (or partial-identification bounds) and a clearly lower confidence grade. **Alternative-interpretation section**: a dedicated discussion that walks the reader through the table in §7.2, states which conclusions survive under each rival regime, and concedes plainly what a belief-dynamics model would add that ours cannot.

### 7.6 Dual welfare criteria

The deepest version of the objection is philosophical rather than empirical: even granting that beliefs drive non-take-up, evaluating welfare at the true $p$ (the paternalistic criterion) is a choice, and a contested one. The defense is to compute everything twice. Under the **belief-respecting criterion** (the planner honors subjective $q$), the internality terms vanish by the envelope logic — marginal households are, by construction, indifferent at their own beliefs. MVPF$_s$ then *loses* a positive term (the crowd-in of mistaken households no longer counts as a welfare gain), while MVPF$_a$ *loses* a negative term (crowd-out no longer counts as a welfare loss). Both changes move the comparison in relief's favor, so the expected pattern — to be verified in the step-1 rerun, not assumed — is that the headline ranking is *preserved or strengthened* under the belief-respecting criterion, while the criterion choice mainly moves the optimal *levels* of intervention. If that expectation holds, the paper can make an unusually strong claim: the case for reallocating the marginal dollar toward relief does not depend on paternalism; only the case for the overall scale of intervention does. Present both criteria side by side in the headline table (framing per Bernheim–Taubinsky's behavioral-welfare treatment), and let the divergence between them be reported as a result in its own right.

## 8. Risks, stated honestly

**Scoop risk on the mix question.** G&S's conclusion, the FEMA wind-down politics, and the visibility of an NBER paper mean the relief margin will attract entrants. Our defense is speed plus the belief machinery, which takes others time to rebuild.

**"G&S plus a belief distribution" perception.** A referee could frame us as an incremental extension. The counter is to lead with what the distribution *does*: the interior optimum where their behavioral case hits a corner, the tail-targeting decomposition, the dampened-crowd-out asymmetry, and the joint optimization. If those are the first things a reader sees, the framing risk is manageable; if the paper opens with the model setup, it is not.

**Belief identification.** The paper's deepest vulnerability; §7 lays out the threat taxonomy, the over-identification tests, and the robustness architecture. The residual risk after all of it: if the fitted $F$ fails the Mulder over-identification check *and* the breakeven contamination share $\chi^*$ turns out to be small, the global exercise loses credibility and the paper must retreat to the local evaluation plus the dual-criteria result — still a paper, but a smaller one.

**Scope creep in the adaptation module.** The July note makes a rich adaptation block feasible — two technologies, discrete heights, two cost margins, a damage-depth distribution — and richness is a trap. The module's job in *this* paper is one instrument (MVPF$_b$), one two-margin insight, and one complementarity proposition; the full inundation-depth machinery is appendix material or a separate quantitative paper. Guard the paper's identity as a public-finance mix paper, not an adaptation engineering paper.

**The corner-solution hazard.** If the corrected numbers deliver MVPF$_a$ ≫ MVPF$_s$ at all reasonable parameters, the paper's answer becomes "cut subsidies, expand relief," which contradicts G&S loudly enough that the reconciliation (their fixed-$f$ assumption, the admin wedge, the internality treatment) must be airtight. If instead the ranking flips under their larger $f$, that is also a publishable finding — but the paper must be written so that either outcome is a result, not an embarrassment.
